## 1. Revenue models in the category and their benchmarks

### Takeaway
The model proven at scale for "grouping strangers" is a paid user subscription. Timeleft reached about €18M ARR with about 150k monthly participants in 2025, roughly 20 months after launch. Venue-side money is modest and usually paid per head:
- Restaurant booking platforms charge about US$1–1.50 (OpenTable) or €2–4 (TheFork) per network cover.
- Nightlife promoters get about US$5–20 per head or 10–35% of bar sales.
- 222 charges venues for foot traffic.

Other benchmarks:
- Ticketing take rates cluster at 5–13%: Luma 5%, Eventbrite about 9.9%, DICE 13% all-in.
- Ticket affiliate commissions are about 6–8% (Fever).
- Sponsorships and B2B are real revenue lines, but the benchmarks found are thin and mostly from vendors.

### Inferences
- **Timeleft's implied revenue per participant.** €18M ARR is about €1.5M a month. Spread over 150k monthly participants, that is roughly €10 per participant per month. This is well below a US$20 single-month list price, which suggests heavy use of multi-month discounts, lower prices in cheaper markets, and/or "participants" including people not paying that month. This is a rough derivation.
- **Timeleft's revenue per employee** is about €180k (€18M / ~100 staff). A low-code, lightweight operating model can support a lean team.
- **Eventbrite's implied 2025 take rate** is at most about 9.7% ($291.8M / >$3.0B gross ticket sales). Eventbrite, a ticketing *tool*, shrank 10% and sold for about $500M. Fever, which owns *demand* through its media and app, raised at $5.2B in September 2026. That contrast supports the idea that owning the audience is worth more than ticketing software. Other causes (product strategy, market) are not controlled for.
- **A plausible revenue stack for a Bogotá "go together with strangers" business**, in the order these precedents suggest:
  1. A user fee: subscription or per-event, possibly as a deposit.
  2. A venue per-head fee, or a percentage of consumption, only for groups the platform brings. The benchmarks are about US$1–1.50 per cover (OpenTable), €2–4 (TheFork) and US$5–20 per head or 10–35% of bar spend (nightlife promoters). These are US/EU figures and need local validation.
  3. Affiliate or ticket commissions of about 5–13% when groups attend third-party ticketed events (6.4–8% Fever affiliate; 5% Luma; about 10% Eventbrite; 13% DICE).
  4. Brand sponsorships once there is a recognizable community.
  5. B2B corporate "social mixers" or team events.
- **Most IRL-friendship players have not monetized the venue side at scale.** Timeleft's is unverified, Pie is free and subsidizes organizers, and Bumble BFF has no revenue. The user side is where proven revenue sits; the venue side looks like a secondary add-on.

- No primary source for Fever's marketplace take rate or venue revenue-share terms; only an aggregator estimate.

## 2. Unit economics for consumer social / IRL businesses

### Takeaway
Hard numbers disclosed by IRL-social companies are scarce. The usable benchmarks are:
- **No-shows:** free events see up to 40–60% no-shows; paid events 8–15%.
- **Subscription conversion:** median download-to-paid is about 2.2% for freemium and 12.1% for hard paywalls; barely 10% of monthly payers reach year two.
- **Paid acquisition:** installs cost about $0.50–2.00 in Latin America vs $2.00–5.50 globally on Meta.
- **Retention:** Pie says retention exceeds 70% once a user has attended three group events.
- **"Success = churn":** this is a known structural problem; Hinge's "designed to be deleted" is the reference case.

### Cited Findings

**No-show rates**
- Eventbrite's organizer blog: no-shows at free events "can be as high as 50%," vs "as low as 10%" at paid events. It recommends adding a paid option (e.g., $10 preferred seating) to raise the perceived value of free tickets. — [Eventbrite AU blog](https://www.eventbrite.com.au/blog/reduce-no-shows-ds0c/); [Eventbrite AU blog 2](https://www.eventbrite.com.au/blog/reduce-no-shows-strategy-ds0c/); [Eventbrite UK blog](https://www.eventbrite.co.uk/blog/how-to-manage-attendance-free-events-ds00/)
- A 2026 industry write-up puts free-event no-shows at 40–60% and paid at 8–15%. — [Addmi](https://addmi.com/blog/reduce-no-shows-association-events)

**Conversion and subscription retention (RevenueCat, all categories)**
- *State of Subscription Apps 2025*: median download-to-paid conversion is 12.1% for hard paywalls vs 2.2% for freemium.
- Median retention figures: 12.8% (hard paywall) vs 9.3% (freemium). The summary does not say which window; it is likely year-one retention of monthly plans.
- "Barely 10% of payers on monthly plans reach the second year."
- The upper quartile retains 60–75% on yearly plans, about twice the median.

  Sources: [RevenueCat SOSA 2025](https://www.revenuecat.com/state-of-subscription-apps-2025); [Subscription Insider](https://www.subscriptioninsider.com/article-type/news/revenuecats-state-of-subscription-apps-2025-report-ais-dominance-retention-challenges-and-the-shift-away-from-pure-subscriptions); [RocketShip HQ](https://www.rocketshiphq.com/revenuecat-state-of-subscription-apps-2025-summary/). ### Inferences
- **Sofar's company share.** Sofar kept about 14% of gross ticket revenue per show after paying artists and costs ($176 / $1,230). Event-operating businesses that pay talent and staff have thin per-event margins unless venue costs are near zero or someone else pays them.
- **Illustrative CAC per paying user** = CPI / install-to-paid conversion. With Latin America CPIs of $0.50–2.00 and conversion of 2–12%, that is roughly US$4–100 per payer. The binding constraints are conversion and repeat attendance, not the cost of the click. This is an illustrative calculation, not a benchmark for this category.
- **A possible North Star metric.** Pie reports >70% retention after three events, and Timeleft says users are happiest at two or more dinners a month. So "share of new users who reach a 2nd and 3rd event within ~30 days" is a plausible activation metric for a Bogotá stranger-group business.
- **Charge something up front.** The 40–60% free vs 8–15% paid no-show gap, together with Pie's model of subsidizing free events, suggests charging a fee or deposit. Otherwise group composition (e.g., six people at a table) collapses.
- **"Success = churn" does not rule out a big business** (Hinge), provided revenue is tied to the ongoing search for new people and experiences rather than to one relationship. For friendship, novelty and variety (venues, formats, new faces) matter for retention. The Timeleft review about repeated restaurants illustrates this.

### Gaps
- No disclosed signup-to-first-event conversion, month-1/month-3 retention, CAC or LTV for Timeleft, 222, Bumble BFF or Meetup.
- No disclosed gross margins for Timeleft, Pie or 222. Eventbrite's gross margin was not retrieved.
- No CAC data specific to Latin America or Colombia for social/IRL apps; only cross-category CPI ranges.
- No data on no-show rates specifically for stranger-matching dinners or group outings, e.g., the effect of Timeleft's prepaid subscription on no-shows.

## 3. Content/audience-first case studies

### Takeaway
The strongest precedent is Fever. It paired an event-discovery app with an owned network of viral city guides (Secret Media: Secret London, Secret NYC, Madrid Secreto and others, with tens of millions of followers). That audience fed ticketing and then Fever's own productions (Candlelight: 3M+ attendees in 2024). By September 2026 Fever had raised $250M at a $5.2B valuation and was EBITDA-positive.

DoStuff (Do512 and others) shows a lightly funded version of the same playbook: comprehensive local listings, then sponsored promotion, ticketing and membership. Do512 reached about $1M in revenue in 2013.

Time Out shows the risk of pure media: media revenue fell 26% in FY2025 as traffic moved to social video and AI search, while its physical Markets grew.

### Inferences
- **Where the precedents made money.** In every successful audience-first case, money came from **transactions and experiences built on the audience**, not from the audience itself: Fever's ticketing and own productions, DoStuff's ticketing and membership, Time Out's Markets. Pure advertising and editorial revenue is the weakest line, and in Time Out's case it is shrinking because of AI search and platform shifts.
- **What an Instagram or TikTok audience can do for a Bogotá business.** A "planes en Bogotá"-style audience can plausibly:
  1. lower CAC through organic reach;
  2. act as a demand-sensing tool (which plans get saves and shares), as Fever did;
  3. earn early revenue through sponsored posts and affiliate ticket commissions (Fever pays 6.4–8%) before the matching product matures.

  The Fever and DoStuff precedents suggest this works best when:
  - the listings are comprehensive and trusted, not only paid placements (DoStuff's principle);
  - the audience is converted into owned channels (app, WhatsApp, email) and own-branded experiences (the Candlelight analogue).
- **Timescales differ.** DoStuff took about seven years to reach roughly $1M in revenue in one city (2006–2013). Timeleft, product-first with paid acquisition and virality and no media arm, reached €1M a month in 16 months. Content-first is capital-efficient but can be slow. A product-first subscription model scaled far faster in the IRL-matching niche specifically.

## 4. The "distribution is the moat in the AI era" thesis, and which moats hold for local IRL networks

### Takeaway
In 2025–2026 a broad set of VCs argue that AI has made software cheap to build and copy, so owned distribution is the durable advantage: audience, community, founder or "founding creator," trust. This group includes GTMfund, a16z (New Media, "momentum is the moat"), Balaji Srinivasan, NFX (distribution as the fast "bailey" layer) and Forbes contributors.

The counterarguments:
- Distribution is "oxygen," not the moat; endurance and deep dependence are.
- Social reach is *rented* and can be repriced by algorithms or AI search, as Time Out's media decline shows.

For local IRL networks, the literature says the real defenses are local density (per-city liquidity) and brand and trust. The weaknesses are:
- network effects that level off once "good enough";
- multi-tenanting, meaning users and venues also use competitors at no cost;
- disintermediation.

### Inferences
- **Matching software is not a moat for a Bogotá stranger-group business.** Timeleft's low-code stack and off-the-shelf quiz-plus-ML matching are easy to copy. What is harder to copy:
  1. **Local liquidity and density:** enough compatible, reliable people in the same neighborhoods, every week. This is a per-city network effect that can level off once "enough" people are present.
  2. **Brand and trust/safety:** strangers must feel safe and that the group will be good.
  3. **Venue relationships:** preferred tables, group deals, off-peak slots.
  4. **Community rituals and habits:** recurring formats, hosts, WhatsApp communities.

  "Distribution" in the sense of an audience is best seen as the fastest way to build (1) and (2), which is the NFX "bailey." It is not a moat by itself, and social-platform reach is rented.
- **Owned channels are the defensible form of distribution.** Channels users opt into (app accounts, WhatsApp lists, email) are more defensible than follower counts, which depend on Meta and TikTok algorithms.
- **Content lowers acquisition cost; it doesn't create lock-in.** Fever's valuation path supports "content-fed demand becomes transactions and owned experiences" as a *compounding* strategy. Pie's move to a creator co-founder and Community Homes, and the VC "founding creator" discourse, show that 2026 practice treats audience-building as a core founding function. The Cathay and Time Out counterpoints, plus multi-tenanting, mean content should be treated as a CAC and trust advantage.

## 5. Disintermediation/leakage and what it implies for monetization

### Takeaway
Research finds that the more a platform builds trust between two parties, the more likely they are to bypass it. For an IRL friendship product, users exchanging WhatsApp numbers is the *intended* outcome, so fees should not depend on the relationship staying on the platform. The models that survive this charge for **ongoing access to new people and curated, organized experiences** (Timeleft's subscription; Hinge's "designed to be deleted" at about $1B), or they host the group itself (Pie's Community Homes). On the venue side, restaurant platforms charge only for platform-sourced ("network") covers and let direct bookings go free. That is a template for charging bars and restaurants only for measurable, incremental groups.

### Cited Findings
- Gu & Zhu (*Management Science*, 2021), a randomized trial on a major online freelance marketplace:
  - Increasing trust raised hiring of high-quality freelancers.
  - When trust was high enough, it also increased disintermediation, which offset the revenue gains.
  - Tendencies to disintermediate varied across users.

  Sources: [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=3182456); [Management Science](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/mnsc.2020.3583)
- "Marketplace Leakage" (*Management Science*, 2023) lists ways to reduce leakage:
  - invest in on-platform transaction benefits;
  - limit communication;
  - charge referral fees;
  - use price-parity clauses;
  - add seller competition;
  - hide sellers who induce leakage.

  Sources: [Management Science](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/mnsc.2023.4757); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/370282409_Marketplace_Leakage)
- Practitioner playbook:
  - trust systems (ratings, verification), guarantees, insurance and secure payments that give reasons to stay;
  - community rules against sharing contact details;
  - NLP monitoring of chats;
  - making the off-platform option visibly riskier ("what protections they forfeit").

  Sources: [Sharetribe](https://www.sharetribe.com/academy/how-to-discourage-people-from-going-around-your-payment-system/); [CometChat](https://www.cometchat.com/blog/platform-leakage); [Cobbleweb](https://www.cobbleweb.co.uk/how-to-prevent-platform-leakage-in-your-online-marketplace/); [LatentView](https://www.latentview.com/blog/how-to-prevent-disintermediation-at-the-marketplace/)
- A 2025 paper studies platform disintermediation with *repeated* transactions (title seen only). — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/397808083_Platform_Disintermediation_with_Repeated_Transactions)
- Dating and friendship analogues:
  - Hinge monetizes the search, not the relationship ("designed to be deleted"; fewer than 15% of users pay; heading toward about $1B). — [Product School](https://productschool.substack.com/p/hinges-cpto-on-building-a-product); [NPR](https://www.npr.org/sections/money/2024/02/13/1228749143/the-dating-app-paradox-why-dating-apps-may-be-worse-than-ever)
  - Pie launched Community Homes in August 2026 as an alternative to group texts and Facebook Groups, to keep groups' social life on its platform. — [TechCrunch, Aug 2026](https://techcrunch.com/2026/08/25/andy-dunns-startup-pie-becomes-less-of-an-events-app-and-more-of-a-social-network/)
  - Bumble relaunched BFF around groups and community. — [Yahoo Finance](https://finance.yahoo.com/news/bumble-bff-revamped-app-focusing-121941463.html)
- Venue-side leakage design:
  - OpenTable charges $1–1.50 per *network* cover but only $0.25 per website cover (or a $49 flat fee). — [OpenTable](https://www.opentable.com/restaurant-solutions/plans/basic/)
  - TheFork's own-website bookings are commission-free. — [Coperti](https://coperti.io/en/blog/restaurant-booking-platform-costs-commission-free-alternatives/)
  - Nightlife promoters are paid per head or as a percentage of tracked door and bar sales, and sub-promoters per client they bring. — [Always The VIP](https://www.alwaysthevip.com/everything-to-know-about-sub-promoters-in-the-nightlife-industry/); [Chron](https://work.chron.com/pay-structure-club-promoter-21106.html)

### Inferences
- **User side.** Leakage (numbers swapped, friendships moving to WhatsApp) cannot be prevented and should not be fought. Pricing that survives it:
  - A subscription or per-event fee for *new* curated groups and experiences. The value is novelty, curation, logistics and safety, which people can't easily get on their own.
  - Memberships in recurring "tribes" or communities that the business hosts, including an official WhatsApp community.
  - Paid add-ons: themed events, premium formats, trips.

  Fees tied to a specific relationship would fail.
- **Venue side.** Leakage risk is that the venue later serves the same people directly. Precedent suggests charging only for platform-sourced groups at the time they visit: a per-head fee, a percentage of spend verified by QR check-in, or a promoter-style arrangement. Direct return visits should not be charged, just as OpenTable and TheFork handle direct covers. Bars may also cut their own deals with groups or with competing organizers (multi-tenanting). Exclusivity is hard to enforce, so the platform's leverage is steady, measurable volume on off-peak nights.
- **Trust investments can raise leakage** (Gu & Zhu). Features that make strangers comfortable, such as verified profiles and host-led formats, will also speed up off-platform friendship. This reinforces pricing access and curation rather than transactions between particular users.

### Gaps
- No quantitative data on leakage rates in IRL friendship or group-event platforms: how many groups keep meeting without the platform, or how that affects retention.
- No documented cases of Latin American venues bypassing event-group platforms, and no local norms for venue exclusivity or per-head deals.
